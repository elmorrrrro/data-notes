**The bombers are not the story. The recipe is.** On June 1, 2025, Ukraine's security service (SBU) launched 117
small drones from trucks parked next to four Russian air bases, while the pilots steered them from Ukraine. The drones
cost about $234,000; Ukraine valued what they hit at $7 billion. But the bigger loss for Moscow was not the aircraft.
It was the discovery that a nuclear power cannot protect its most guarded assets from something that costs less than
a used car. Below: how that recipe spread, why it now scares Washington, and who on the US stock market is paid to
copy it.

## 1. A recipe, not a raid

<ReachMap />

The bases were spread over four time zones; the farthest, Belaya in Siberia, is 4,300 km from Ukraine's border. Its
bombers are the ones that launch cruise missiles at Ukrainian cities, and Russia has not built a new Tu-22M3 since
1993. The drones did not fly that far: they were driven there in secret, after 18 months of planning. That is the
point. As Vitaliy Portnikov, the Ukrainian political commentator, put it, the operation happened and "no one in the
Russian security services knew about it". Distance, air defence and the secret police all failed against a truck.

Twelve days later the recipe was used again. On June 13, 2025, Israel's Mossad launched drones from secret bases it had
set up inside Iran, against air defences near Tehran, at the start of Israel's air campaign. Portnikov's verdict: the
two services "created a universal recipe for destroying the security of a nuclear state". Ukraine kept using its own
version the long way, by air: in 2026 its long-range drones hit **Primorsk** and **Ust-Luga**, Russia's biggest oil
ports on the Baltic, 850–920 km from the border. After the March strikes Primorsk had lost at least 40% of its oil
storage, according to satellite images analysed for Reuters. That is money that pays for the war.

## 2. Why America now wants the Ukrainian price

<PriceLadder />

Portnikov's next line is the one that matters for the rest of this study: "What Vladimir Putin should fear, Donald
Trump should fear too." Every US base, ship and airport is as exposed to a cheap drone as Belaya was, and the US
arsenal is built on the opposite logic. A Ukrainian FPV drone costs about **$400**; a Javelin, the US anti-tank
missile, cost the US Army $192,772 per round in its 2022 budget, **482 times** more.

Washington reacted fast. Five days after Spiderweb, President Trump signed an executive order titled "Unleashing
American Drone Dominance". In July 2025 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered that by the end of 2026 every squad
gets "low-cost, expendable drones", calling them "the biggest battlefield innovation in a generation". In 2026 the
Army asked industry for FPV drones under $2,000 each and the Marines for 10,000 under $4,000. Both ceilings are still
five to ten times what Ukraine pays, and the cheap parts that would close the gap mostly come from China.

## 3. Who gets the money

<SinceSpiderweb />

The cheapest drones in the world are made by hundreds of private Ukrainian companies, which you can't buy on an
exchange. In the US, four listed names are tied to small drones: **Unusual Machines** makes FPV parts, including
motors; **Red Cat** makes small drones for the US Army; **AeroVironment** makes Switchblade; **Lockheed Martin** makes
Javelin with RTX, the missile that FPVs undercut.

Only one beat the market, and it doesn't sell the finished weapon. Unusual Machines is up 341% since Spiderweb, while
the S&P 500 rose 32%. Its pitch is exactly the gap from section 2: drone parts that are not made in China. In May 2026
the Wall Street Journal reported that the Pentagon was in talks to fund it, possibly for an ownership stake; the stock jumped
37% before the market even opened. With $11 million of revenue in 2025, Unusual Machines is priced on Washington's
plans, not on its sales. The weapon makers did worse than the index: AeroVironment is down 22%, Red Cat and Lockheed
Martin are up 3%.

My reading: Spiderweb changed the idea of security, and America has started paying for it. So far the market rewards
whoever can replace Chinese parts, not whoever builds the cheapest drone. The cheapest drones are still Ukrainian.

## What the numbers leave out

- **Price is not cost per hit.** Many FPV drones are jammed or miss, and a Javelin is far more likely to hit. There
  are no public hit rates, so the ladder compares prices, not results.
- **Spiderweb's $234,000 is only the drones.** It leaves out the trucks, 18 months of planning and the people. The
  $7 billion is Ukraine's own valuation of the aircraft; independent counts are lower (two US officials told Reuters
  about 20 hit and 10 destroyed).
- **Distance is not flight range.** Spiderweb's drones were driven close to the bases. The map measures how far the
  operation reached from Ukraine's border, not how far a quadcopter flies. The ports were hit by long-range drones,
  a different and more expensive type.
- **Prices come from different years and sources.** The Javelin and Switchblade figures are US Army budget lines for
  fiscal 2022; the FPV price is a typical figure from press reports; the US targets are ceilings, not contract prices.
- **Share prices have many causes.** Contracts, earnings, Pentagon plans and the whole market moved these stocks; the
  chart does not show that Spiderweb moved them. Javelin is a small part of Lockheed Martin's $75 billion of sales.
- **Portnikov is a commentator, not a source of data.** His quotes frame the argument; every number here comes from the
  sources below.
